---
title: The Tale of Rappan Athuk
publish: true
---

Long ago, ere the rise of humanity, when dwarves and elves contended with dragons and ruled great empires, all the lands from the sea to the [[Great Rift]] were held by the line of the Deepkings[^1]. These wise and pious dwarves oversaw an empire of their own people, but also of many others who thrived under their just rule. The Deepking could but beckon and the realms of the world would gather to his call, to treat and answer perils they faced in common.   

At the end of that age, Graeltor[^2] sat the Mithril Throne. Word came to him by many paths[^3], about the rise of a wicked temple-city of [[Orcus]] and its growing depredations. From its towers spun necromancies such as the world had never seen. From its gates marched legions beyond count, their march shaded by the wings of dragons. And from its depths issued teeming hordes of orc and goblin, covering the land like a plague of locusts. The blood of ten nations flowed across its altars in sacrifices and desolation spread from its black walls. A growing shadow reached from [[Peleveran]] across the plains, threatening to engulf all the Realms.  

Thus Graeltor called the nations of the world to his throne, for a last alliance of the Proud Peoples[^4]. But the councils of the just were troubled and every realm demanded the honor of leadership for themselves alone. Even among the dwarves, each clan clamored for one of its own. The effort to answer the hateful temple-city by force of arms may have failed in that moment, but for Graeltor’s wisdom. He chose an outsider, a human known to him but not beholden to any empire or closely tied to any particular faith. Thus did [[Zelkor]], a mage of uncertain origin[^5], come to lead the host against Peleveran.   

The battles of Zelkor’s host are the stuff of legend. Through strength of arms, great Art, and divine powers called down by legions of priests of many faiths, the forces of Peleveran were thrown back against their black walls. From those walls, great engines belched forth clouds of magical gas, blasts of deadly flame and lightning, and black rays of death itself. Both sides incurred massive losses, but Zelkor’s army of light at least invested Peleveran and began the slow work of reducing its walls. However, by unknown means Orcus’ hordes quit their walls and escaped the siege. They retreated into the west.  

Zelkor gave chase, wary but unwilling to allow Orcus’ forces to scatter and rise anew. The armies clashed thrice more in pitched battle, but each time the forces of evil managed to elude full capture. Finally they reached a tangled forest near to the sea. Zelkor grandly proclaimed the wood the Forest of Hope, for it would witness the final destruction of Orcus’ might. His army followed them beneath the boughs.   

Both armies vanished to the last. Not a single survivor staggered out from beneath the trees. Nor did they leave behind any trace for those who sought them in vain.  

The sudden loss of so many capable and well-loved leaders, both of nations and faiths, led to generations of revolt and strife that much diminished the prestige and might of all the powers of the age. None suffered more from it than the Deep Realm, where Graeltor wept for his lost legions. The despair, it is said, unsettled his mind and he would often thereafter speak as if Zelkor was before him and demand to know what had become of his people.[^6]  

Future generations could take consolation, at least, from the slow rise of newer, more vigorous powers and point with some satisfaction to the fact that Orcus’ cult did not again rise to prominence. In time, they argued that while the losses were painful they had at least achieved their goal. Others darkly countered that the loss of so many mighty champions of many nations and faiths seemed calculated to weaken the powers of the age, as indeed it did. Even the [[Jundarwood|Forest of Hope]], though long avoided, seemed now no more than a tangled wood near the ocean.  

And for an age the forest remained quiet. The strange and tragic losses within it fell out of living memory, into the histories of shorter-lived races, and thence to legend. Humans and others ventured, first tentatively but soon with increasing confidence into the tangled wood. They forgot its ill-starred name and dubbed it the [[Jundarwood]][^7]. Foresters and druids took up residence and, while it remained wild, the wood’s relative placidity slowly turned tales of it being the home to a fell evil, or accursed, into stories to frighten children with.  

Then the peaceful creatures of the wood began to disappear. Rangers and druids who made it their business to see what caused this often themselves vanished, though the few who emerged again reported that a dark mausoleum and cemetery now stood within the forest. These tidings drew the eye of [[Bashir yn Daud el Anachtyr]], leader of the Shields of Almraiven[^8], a  powerful adventuring fellowship dedicated to the Triad[^9].  

The Shields ventured into the Jundarwood early in the Year of the Bright Sun[^10] and reached the mausoleum. Beneath it sprawled a vast labyrinth. They descended and battled great hordes of wicked foes and at great length. Their number dwindled, as did their magic and supplies, until Bashir led a group of four. Three of those escaped. Bashir was lost.  

The survivors’ tales of a vast complex dedicated to evil, perilous beyond imagination, suggested also riches and fame beyond imagination. All could be had within the Jundarwood, drawing ranks of adventurers, many of whom have struck it rich in the effort. Many more have vanished, dying horribly, or returned scarred and maimed of body and mind, from one of the many entrances to the seemingly endless labyrinth. In time they came to call the mausoleum and the sprawling dungeon beneath it [[Rappan Athuk]][^11], or the Dungeon of Graves.

[^1]:  Deepking is the most common Shaaran translation of the dwarven term Ardornar and is generally considered polite and respectful. It is also sometimes rendered as Overthane, Overking, or High King. Learned dwarves tend to find the term High King humorousHalruaan scribes, and those influenced by them, sometimes render it as Archking. Calishite authorities simply use their own term, Syl-Pasha. Most dwarves find the latter practice decidedly rude. The title has only ever been broadly recognized for two dwarven realms, of which only the present Deep Kingdom of the Great Rift survives. 

[^2]:  Graeltor’s precise clan is disputed, privately and sometimes heatedly, among dwarves themselves. 

[^3]:  Competing traditions credit the news to representatives of each prominent race, realm, and faith. These reliably cite their own as the first to arrive and have the others show up just in time to confirm the account. “Many paths” is the diplomatic way to avoid the question.

[^4]:  A mostly-human term for elves and dwarves, collectively.

[^5]:  Zelkor’s true origins were, it is said, known only to himself, Graeltor, and the gods. While most agree he was in fact human, he may have hailed from another world. A minority suggest that his humanity was a magical disguise cloaking his true nature as a dragon. A few bold souls think he may have been a disguised yuan-ti or mind flayer who concealed the fact from Graeltor or addled the Ardornar’s wits so as to secure his appointment.

[^6]:  Whether Graeltor truly suffered a mental break in despair or this is poetic license has been lost to history, but there’s little debate over dating the ebb of dwarven power and influence to the debacle at the Forest of Hope. Suppositions that a curse befell the Deep Realm, or dwarves in general, as a result are common in non-dwarven circles. Airing them in the presence of dwarves is unwise.

[^7]:  The Jundarwood is in the Border Kingdoms, to the northeast and on the far side of the Shaar.

[^8]:  Almraiven is a city in eastern Calimshan known for its mages. The Shields’ activities were concentrated in Calimshan, Tethyr, and around the Lake of Steam. Their exploits include the slaying of several liches, the exposure of the then-current ruler of Ankhapur as a disguised beholder, and a very destructive battle against a trio of Red Wizards in the skies above Innarlith. The last resulted in the restoration of much of the damage by means of multiple miracle spells, the combined toll of which aged Brother Jabir el-Ilmater nearly to the point of death. Upon discovering that several children died in the clash, the prematurely-aged Brother Jabir looked skyward, bid his companions in arms a fond farewell, and spent the last of life to restore them.

[^9]:  The closely allied human gods Tyr, Ilmater, and Torm

[^10]:  One hundred and seventeen years ago.

[^11]:  Rappan Athuk is not a translation of Dungeon of Graves, but rather the name by which its inhabitants refer to it among themselves and which has slowly passed into common knowledge in the decades since the dungeon’s discovery.
