The historical details of the following tale are a matter of some dispute. Among sages of the younger races, [[Abgrimm]] is treated less as a historical figure than as an archetype: the Tragic Dwarf, noble scion of a people in decline, whose passing may be admired but need not be mourned overmuch. He features prominently in the romantic and elegiac ballads of non-dwarven bards, particularly those of gnomish composition, whose fondness for such tales has done much to shape the popular conception of him. 

Singing these ballads in the presence of dwarves is a fraught business. Many take exception even to sympathetic, admiring renditions on various grounds. The most common explanation for dwarven dislike is that non-dwarves have no right to speak, even in admiration, of such a noble dwarf. He is not theirs to remember. Others suspect that the true objection, perhaps unacknowledged even by the dwarves themselves, is that such performances risk moving their listeners to un-dwarven expressions of grief.

The rare tendency to recast Abgrimm as a comic protagonist, blundering through misadventures and wandering lost in the fog of his own senility, provokes a far more vigorous response, on occasion a violent one. Whether a historical Abgrimm ever existed is, to the scholars of the younger races, largely beside the point. The legend has long since outgrown any dwarf who might have inspired it.

The dwarves of the [[Channath Vale]] know otherwise: Abgrimm was a real dwarf, though they disagree among themselves, and strictly in private, as to which clan may rightfully claim him. To dispute such matters in the hearing of outsiders would be to lend credence to the suggestion that Abgrimm was an invention in the first place, a concession no self-respecting dwarf would make.

The dwarves of [[Rethmar]] possess accounts of Abgrimm dating back roughly five centuries, though, following repeated attempts at theft, they no longer permit outsiders to examine them. More may be held beyond the adamantine gates, in the Deep Realm itself. Such places are closed to all save dwarves, who consider the matter of Abgrimm's historicity settled and see no need to furnish further evidence.

### The Tale of Abgrimm the Last[^1]

In ages past, Rethmar was a city of the Morndinsamman, its temples raised in honor of the dwarven gods and its halls alive with the clangor of hammer upon anvil. Its people were many, its craft renowned, and its dead were laid to rest in the Valley of Silent Dwarves, where Moradin and Berronar kept watch over the gem-studded tombs of heroes. There, beneath stone vaults carved to endure until the world's ending, the ancestors awaited the day when their descendants would join them. The mountains stood, the gods watched, and the dwarves believed that what had been wrought with patience and devotion would endure forever.

But the years passed, and the tide of Men[^2] rose against the old order. The dwarves of Rethmar grew fewer, their lineages thinning, their workshops falling quiet one by one. Great halls stood empty beneath ceilings their builders had carved with scenes of victories no living dwarf could remember. The fires cooled in forges that had once shaped wonders, and the names of master craftsmen passed from common speech into the inscriptions on their tombs. 

Meanwhile, the city beyond those ancient halls changed. Men filled its streets and raised their own houses against dwarven stonework, until Rethmar became a human city built upon dwarven foundations. The old temples endured, but their congregations shrank, and one by one their doors were closed. Their priests withdrew to the remaining sanctuaries, where the faithful gathered in ever smaller numbers. The [[Valley of Silent Dwarves]][^3] remained, its tombs unbroken, a site of increasingly rare pilgrimages.

In time, even the surviving congregations of Rethmar had dwindled to a remnant. Abgrimm, High Forgesmith[^4] of the last great temple, sat in a chamber where once a throne of mithril had stood. He was old beyond the reckoning of Men[^5], his beard white as winter stone and his shoulders bent beneath the weight of years. He had buried generations of the faithful. He had watched young priests take up the duties of their elders, and had seen those same priests carried to their rest while their successors grew old in turn. Now fewer than ten underpriests remained to tend the temple, and each year brought fewer candles to illuminate its altars.

Abgrimm continued the rites as they had been performed since the world's youth. He spoke the ancient invocations, recited the names of the honored dead, and offered prayers before the images of gods whose worship had once filled the hall with voices. His words rose beneath the vaulted ceiling and returned to him unanswered[^6], though he never ceased to speak them. The gods had not died. The mountains had not fallen. The tombs of the ancestors remained secure beneath the earth. It was the dwarves themselves who were passing away, and no hammer could mend what time had broken.

At last, Abgrimm understood that his own end was near. He prayed long into the dark, kneeling before the altars with joints that creaked like old timber. When he bowed his head, strands of his beard fell upon the stone, and he knew they would never grow again. Then, in the stillness before dawn, he received what he understood to be a summons from the Morndinsamman. The gods called him to the Valley of Silent Dwarves.

He summoned his underpriests, and told them that he would make the journey alone. They must not grieve for him, he said, nor surrender themselves to despair. He would return, and with him would come the restoration of the dwarven people. So he spoke, and the young priests listened, for though they had never known the glory of the old days, they had heard their elders speak of them, and they wished to believe that such glory might yet return. Abgrimm laid aside his hammer of office and departed for the valley.

He was never seen again.

In time, the underpriests sent a company to seek Abgrimm, guarded by a band of devout dwarven. They searched the ancient paths and entered the valley, where the tombs of heroes lay beneath their jeweled ceilings. But the shrines had been defiled.[^7] The sanctuaries were profaned, offerings scattered, and the signs of desecration lay where generations of dwarves had knelt in reverence. Of Abgrimm there was no trace: no body, no broken weapon, no final inscription upon the stone. The valley had received him, or else he had never reached it. No one could say.[^8]

The gnomish bard suggests that Abgrimm received no summons from the gods, and that no promise of restoration had been granted to him. Knowing the end had come, the old priest departed so that his underpriests would not have to lay their beloved patriarch to rest with the rites befitting his station. The expense would have consumed what little wealth remained to the temple, leaving the survivors utterly destitute. Thus, the bard imagines, Abgrimm went alone into the valley, surrendering even his soul to spare those who remained.

These verses are seldom sung before dwarves. The suggestion that Abgrimm, a patriarch of their faith, would knowingly speak falsely of the gods is a grave insult, and many consider the ballad's final speculation an act of cruelty disguised as poetry. Yet the sorrow that precedes it is familiar to all who have seen the old halls. For the dwarves know that stone endures. Their halls may stand until the mountains crumble, their treasures outlast kingdoms, and their ancestors' names remain carved upon the tombs long after all who spoke them have turned to dust. But stone cannot keep a people alive. The forges cool, the songs are forgotten, and the great halls stand empty beneath their unchanging vaults. The gods remain. The mountains remain. And the dwarves grow fewer.[^9]

[^1]:  Some prefer a gentler rendering, known as *Abgrimm the Lost*, which leaves room for hope where the original offers none.

[^2]:  The tide of Men here is metaphorical, rather than reference to a specific invasion or war. The empire of the dwarves contended with many foes in its prime, including giants and dragons, as well as humans. The tide is a slow decline over many generations of humans and rather fewer of dwarves, punctuated by various wars and calamities. The most famous of these is the loss of Overking Grailtor’s Army of Light, which legend holds encompassed all the nations of Realms in righteous might assembled, laying siege to a city of great wickedness, and then chasing their foes into a thick forest from which none returned. Some scholars of the Abgrimm as Noble Dwarf school of thought believe his tale is a retelling of the fate of Grailtor’s legend.

[^3]:  Sometimes referred to as the Valley of the Shrines, Stoneheart Valley, the Brother Gods’ Vale, and the Dwarves’ Vale.

[^4]:  High Forgesmith is a title reserved for Moradin’s most exalted clergy, only to be bestowed by a council of existing High Forgesmiths or the god himself. No High Forgesmith is known to exist outside the Deep Realm since Abgrimm’s time.

[^5]:  While allowing for poetic license, it’s generally thought that Abgrimm was truly ancient even by dwarven reckoning. He may have seen nearly six hundred years.

[^6]:  Dwarves generally take great exception to the suggestion that a High Forgesmith could ever utter a prayer without answer from their gods. 

[^7]:  The sources and nature of this desecration vary greatly. Popular culprits include grasping adventurers, drow, duergar, and writhing horrors the crawled up from the Underdark. Cautious performers cast the blame vaguely to “the enemies of the dwarves”.

[^8]:  Wise performers, in the presence of dwarves, end the story with the desecration and Abgrimm’s disappearance.

[^9]:  Whether they end the story with Abgrimm lost and desecration uncovered in the Valley of Silent Dwarves or accept a longer rendition, dwarves prefer to end the story with the following traditional prayer “The secret fires of hearts burn with the memory of wars lost and heroes slain, but like the stone from which we came, we endure.”
