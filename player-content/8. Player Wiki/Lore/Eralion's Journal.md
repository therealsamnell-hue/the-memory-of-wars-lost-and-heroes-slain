Eralion's Journal is a well-worn, leatherbound volume, its pages yellowed and occasionally stained with ink, reagents, and the accumulated detritus of a wizard's working life. Its entries are irregular, sometimes separated by months or even years, and vary considerably in tone. The earlier passages are written in a rather earnest hand, while the later ones become increasingly assured, elaborate, and self-important. Taken together, they provide a remarkably intimate account of the wizard's life, ambitions, and fate.

The earliest entries concern Eralion's youth in Sheirtalar and his apprenticeship under Vordal of the Emerald Rings, a dwarf of Clan Gordrivver. Vordal was already of considerable age when Eralion came into his service, well into his fourth century. The young apprentice writes of his master's various infirmities, his declining vigor, and, more frequently, his profound sorrow at the state of the dwarven people. The halls of the dwarves were emptying, their old glories fading, and Vordal seemed unable to speak of much else. Eralion's early affection for his master is evident, though it is occasionally tempered by frustration. He complains in one entry of hearing the same lamentations repeated for the hundredth time, and of the difficulty of finding some fresh diversion with which to raise the old dwarf's spirits. 

One of the few things that evidently pleased Vordal was Eralion's deep devotion to Moradin. The dwarf regarded this as a particularly admirable quality in his pupil, although his enthusiasm cooled considerably whenever Eralion attributed his faith to some distant dwarven ancestry. This clearly became a sore point for both over time.

Eralion's apprenticeship began in the Year of the Dark Maskl[^1] and the amount of entries and references within them to recent events suggest it continued at least through the Year of Sinking Sails[^2].

There is no account of the formal conclusion of Eralion's apprenticeship. Several years after the last entry concerning Vordal, however, the journal records the young wizard's return to find that he had arrived too late. Age had at last claimed the elderly dwarf.

Eralion's subsequent attempts to return Vordal's personal effects to Clan Gordrivver in person were met with an unexpectedly cold reception. He was instructed to convey the belongings to a dwarven merchant, and admonished in the strongest possible terms that he was not to present himself at the gates of a Gordrivver dwarfhold, much less presume to beg admittance. The rejection clearly wounded him. Several subsequent entries are devoted to religious speculation, questions of inheritance and belonging, and lengthy, rather overwrought prayers to Moradin. The young wizard seems particularly troubled by the apparent contradiction between his master's affection for him and the contempt shown by his master's kin.

More than a decade passes before the next entry.

The Eralion who emerges from this gap is a very different man: confident, worldly, and firmly established in his profession. He records service with three different adventuring companies before finally accumulating sufficient wealth to hire companions of his own. He finds this arrangement considerably more agreeable, as it allows him to determine the priorities of the company without having to suffer the competing ambitions and questionable judgment of others.

It is during this period that he records his first meeting with Feriblan of Kormul, on an initial expedition into the Channath Vale. Feriblan becomes a valued acquaintance, mentioned several times with fondness in subsequent years.

A recurring theme of these entries is Eralion's dissatisfaction with his own accomplishments. Though plainly successful by the standards of most adventuring wizards, he is vexed by the suspicion that he has not yet become a mage of sufficient renown to be a credit to Vordal's memory. He writes often of the great works of legendary archmages and wonders whether his own name will ever be remembered alongside theirs.

In time, however, he accumulates sufficient wealth and materials to undertake a project of considerable ambition. Having gathered an extraordinary quantity of reagents, he performs a grand working to summon a small host of earth elementals, which he sets to the construction of a keep according to his own designs. The entire structure is raised in the space of a single day and night.

Eralion is initially delighted with the result, but his satisfaction proves short-lived. In an unusually candid observation, he admits that he likely spent as much ordinary Art correcting and refining the elementals' work as he would have spent had he simply supervised a more deliberate construction. He concludes, somewhat grudgingly, that patience might have served him better than ambition. This keep, which he names Fairkeep, becomes his home as he retires from the adventuring life to devote himself more fully to the Art.

During the early years of his retirement, Eralion meets Gethrame. She is a frequent visitor to Fairkeep, and he in turn makes regular journeys to see her in Drellin's Ferry. Their early acquaintance appears to have been founded upon a mutual interest in magical theory and religious philosophy. The journal records increasingly lengthy discussions between them, and Eralion's evident pleasure in having found someone with whom he might speak freely of such matters.

When their affection eventually becomes something more than intellectual, Eralion remarks that the details are properly the business of the watching gods. For a time, the entries suggest that he has found a happiness that neither wealth nor magical accomplishment had afforded him. His writing becomes less concerned with his own reputation and more with the pleasures of companionship.

This period of contentment does not last.

The village priestess curses Gethrame for some supposed trespass involving scrying upon secret rites. Eralion is adamant that the offense was either entirely imaginary or so trivial as to make the punishment monstrous. The priestess blinds Gethrame and casts her into exile. Eralion's outrage is immediate and considerable, but his attempts to discover what has become of his beloved initially prove fruitless. She has fled the village without leaving word of her destination.

For more than a tenday he writes nothing. When he does resume his journal, it is with barely concealed elation. Through divination, he has discovered Gethrame's whereabouts: a cave not far from Fairkeep. He goes to her at once and promises that he will find a means of restoring her sight, and that he shall accomplish this without placing his trust in the inconstant gods who have permitted such an injustice.

His subsequent entries describe a journey to Rethmar, where he contracts with an elven craftsman named Velior to fashion a finely wrought staff capable of bearing considerable enchantment. With the staff completed, Eralion returns to Fairkeep and begins preparations for the working by which he intends to restore Gethrame's vision.

One entry, in particular, is written with unusual vehemence:

_"I shall discard any question of prudence, in the face of manifest necessity and the compassion to which my love is so fulsomely due. The sacrifice required is from a vile creature, this scurrying, diseased shamaness. It is meet that she worships the Plague-Crone, for she imitates in her very nature that dark goddess."_

The precise nature of the working is not fully explained, though the journal makes clear that Eralion intends to exact some form of sacrifice from the priestess responsible for Gethrame's affliction.

Gethrame is delighted with the new staff, which permits her to resume her scryings. Eralion shares her joy, but only for a time. He grows increasingly dismissive of Gethrame's divinations, describing them as foolish soothsayings, and begins to question her competence with the Art. What had once been a source of mutual fascination becomes, in his writing, an intolerable nuisance.

Eventually, he declares that he can no longer endure her company.

_"I have no choice but to leave her. I cannot stand to witness what she has become, nor hear again her senseless ramblings. Truly Savras must have blessed her in the same fashion as Chauntea did, only striking her wits instead of her eyes. I leave her to whatever her fate may be."_

There is no further mention of Gethrame.

From this point onward, the journal becomes a rather different document. Eralion writes more frequently, and with a growing sense of his own importance. His former references to Moradin, once so earnest and numerous, disappear entirely. In their place are increasingly elaborate meditations on the nature and limits of the Art, and the injustice of mortal existence.

His chief grievance is now the brevity of his own life. He considers it an absurdity that a wizard of his considerable talents should be allotted so few years in which to pursue his studies, while other practitioners of the Art have enjoyed centuries. The comparison with dwarves is particularly galling. Even a common dwarven blacksmith, he observes, may expect to outlive a human wizard of considerable accomplishment by generations. The unfairness of this arrangement seems to him self-evident.

His initial researches into magic capable of extending his lifespan prove disappointing. He dismisses enchanted pools as likely to be the stuff of legend, and regards elixirs of longevity as unreliable, notwithstanding his own experiments with them during his youth. Nor does he possess the means to sustain an extended existence in some longer-lived form.

He briefly considers dedicating himself to Mystra, the Mother of All Magic, but rejects the notion as an unnecessarily elaborate means of surrendering his will and existence to a goddess in exchange for an eternity of toilsome service. He seems to find the prospect of such an arrangement considerably less appealing than the prospect of simply continuing his own work indefinitely.

One possibility, however, captures his attention.

The so-called mushrooms of youth are mentioned in several independent accounts, some of them sufficiently credible to attract his serious interest. These rare growths are said to occur only within the walls of [[The Tale of Rappan Athuk|Rappan Athuk]], the Dungeon of Graves. In the Year of the Wandering Maiden[^3], he makes the journey to that ill-starred place. He does not return with the mushrooms he sought. Instead, he comes back with discoveries that he considers considerably more valuable.

The precise nature of these discoveries is not fully explained in the journal, though the final entries make clear that Eralion believes he has found a means to transcend the ordinary limitations of mortal life.

The last entries are dated to the Year of the Weeping Moon[^4]. Eralion writes that he has sent a letter to his old acquaintance Feriblan of Kormul, relating something of his discoveries. He intends to delay his final undertaking only a little longer, partly to ensure that all preparations are complete, and partly, it seems, in the hope that Feriblan might arrive in time to witness his triumph.

The absence of a reply irritates him considerably. He speculates on the reasons for Feriblan's silence, but ultimately dismisses the matter as unimportant. The work is ready, and he has no intention of allowing the indifference or tardiness of others to delay him any further.

His final entry is written in a tone of scarcely restrained excitement. He resolves to keep the journal close at hand, so that he might record his sensations upon first awakening to his new and everlasting existence.
